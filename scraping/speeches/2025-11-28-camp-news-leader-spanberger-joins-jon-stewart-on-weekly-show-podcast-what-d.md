By Lyra Bordelon

Governor-elect Abigail Spanberger joined comedian John Stewart on his podcast, The Weekly Show, and reflected on her time in Congress, the governor campaign trail, and the effects of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act on Virginia.

Stewart was joined by Spanberger and New Jersey Governor-elect Mikie Sherrill for an episode released Nov. 21. The pair spoke on and joked about their time as neighbors and roommates while serving in Congress. Spanberger represented Virginia’s 7th Congressional District for three terms before running for governor.

Both governors-elect expressed frustration with dysfunction in Congress. Spanberger reflected on her time pushing for the Social Security Fairness Act, which former president Joe Biden signed at the beginning of the year. The bill eliminated two previous provisions that reduced Social Security benefits for retirees who received pensions or worked in the public sector, including police officers, firefighters, and public school teachers.

“People would say ‘I support it, I support it,’ they’d co-sponsor it, we’d have all the co-sponsors, but then, year after year, we couldn’t get leadership, Democratic or Republican, to bring it for a vote,” Spanberger said. Instead, the bill was ultimately pushed through via a discharge petition, bypassing leadership.

The trio talked about political advertisements during the race. Spanberger said the ads affected her more during her first race for office but they don’t affect her as much now. When they release, she watches them first with the sound off as a coping mechanism. She also said the ads affect her mother more than her.

“While a lot of these negative ads were out there, I always thought it was important to have positive, bio ‘what am I for’ ads, because you can’t just be about ‘vote against this person,'” Spanberger said. “We’re trying to deliver an economic message on affordability.”

Spanberger also discussed the ramifications of H.R. 1, also known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, a federal spending bill passed by Republicans and signed by President Donald Trump in July, including its effects on healthcare. She pointed to the closure of three Augusta Health clinics and said that many hospitals in the state that serve rural Virginia communities do not technically qualify as rural hospitals for federal financial support.

“I think its a total lack of understanding about how the basics of government works and a willingness of Republican legislators, and I’ll call out the ones in Virginia, to vote for a bill that rural hospital leadership in their district … were just writing op/ed after op/ed, were waiving red flags saying danger danger danger this is going to hurt us,” Spanberger said. “In fact, some of the communities that will be most hurt by that bill in particular are the ones that are represented by Republicans in Congress.”

In September, Sixth District Representative Ben Cline ‘lamented’ the August Health closures during a town hall in Lexington. He celebrated his vote for H.R. 1, saying Medicaid was not being cut but was instead instituting work requirements for “able-bodied” adults.

Spanberger also addressed the University of Virginia’s search for a new president following the resignation of James Ryan. In November, she wrote a letter to the university, saying she was “deeply concerned” about the community’s confidence in the Board of Visitors, the 17-member UVA leadership board, and called Ryan’s departure the “result of federal overreach.”

Ryan, in his own post-resignation letter, said the Department of Justice opened an investigation into the university and offered the university concessions if Ryan stepped down. He did not speak on it publicly at the time, saying it felt like a “hostage situation, where the kidnapper threatens harm if you do not keep information about the demands confidential.” He wrote the fight began over the university’s policies over diversity, equity and inclusion. According to Ryan, the Department of Justice suggesting “that we were supposed to eliminate the entirety of DEI.”

Ryan’s full letter was published in full by C-VILLE Weekly. Stewart and Spanberger complimented Ryan’s letter.

“When people question, ‘why is it that the university or the president didn’t defend themselves,’ [it is] because university counsel was hand picked by our state attorney general, who’s doing the bidding for the Department of Justice,” said Spanberger.

Of the 17-member leadership board, five of the seats are currently open. Spanberger said her goal was to appoint members who could work toward “steadying the ship.”
