# COMPOSITE CONSTITUENT PROFILES — ENERGY AFFORDABILITY

Prepared for listening-session prep. Policy desk, August 2026.
Four composites. Each one is a real *question* we keep getting, wearing a face.

---

## 1. DENISE HARTWELL — retired, Northern Neck (COMPOSITE)

Age 71. Widowed, owns her house outright, lives on Social Security and a small pension.
Electric bill is the single largest variable line in her month, and she has started
managing it by not running the heat pump in shoulder seasons.

**What she asks:** "Everyone keeps telling me about programs. Is any of it going to
show up on my bill, or is it all for people who can afford to install something?"

**What we actually have for her:**
- The **RGGI Consumer Credit** — a direct credit for residential customers, built into
  the budget, designed to offset the RGGI charge; estimates that average monthly bills
  could drop by as much as **$36 annually**
- **VE Connect**, the one-stop shop for programs and incentives
- The **transmission order** — she will never see a line item that says so, but she is
  no longer paying toward lines built for someone else's data center

**Where we're weak with her:** Switch Together is a poor fit — she is not financing a
solar installation at 71 on a fixed income, and pitching it to her sounds like we
didn't listen. Don't lead with it. Lead with the credit and VE Connect.

---

## 2. MARCUS OYELARAN — small manufacturer, Southside (COMPOSITE)

Owns a 34-person fabrication shop. Energy is his third-largest cost. He is not
ideological about any of this; he is trying to quote jobs twelve months out without
knowing what power will cost.

**What he asks:** "I'm not asking anyone to save me. I'm asking what my rate looks like
in three years so I can bid work."

**What we have:**
- RGGI credit applies to **small businesses**, not just households
- **Switch Together** covers **business** installations too — 23% average saving,
  ~$6,300 on a typical system, plus battery storage discounts
- Consumption tax and transmission cost allocation both push large-load costs onto
  large loads rather than onto him

**The honest limit:** we cannot promise him a rate three years out. What we can say is
that every structural lever we've pulled — consumption tax, but-for transmission
allocation, PJM engagement, storage goals — moves cost off ratepayers like him. That's
a direction, not a guarantee, and he'll respect the distinction more than a dodge.

---

## 3. TANYA BREWSTER — Dominion lineworker, Central Virginia (COMPOSITE)

19 years on the job. Reads about the **$67 billion NextEra merger** in the paper. Her
question is not about rates.

**What she asks:** "Is my job in Virginia in five years, and who decides that — you, or
a company in Florida?"

**What we can say, precisely:**
- Protecting Virginia's utility workforce is **one of the three non-negotiables**, and
  the Governor named this work specifically in the Washington Post — *linemen who get
  the lights back on after a rough storm, grid operators, engineers*
- Intervening makes the administration a **party** to the SCC case, with the legal
  right to request information and raise concerns
- **The SCC decides**, not the Governor

**What we cannot say:** that anyone's job is guaranteed. Do not let a staffer drift
into implying it. The credible version is: someone will be in the room asking this
question on the record, which was not true before August 6.

**Note for advance:** the workforce non-negotiable is the one we have said the most
about and shown the least of. A composite like Tanya is exactly who should be standing
behind the Governor at the next energy event, and we don't currently have that
relationship built. Flagged in the merger posture memo as an open item.

---

## 4. RAY AND JUDITH COMBS — Loudoun-adjacent, data center corridor (COMPOSITE)

Farm land in the family three generations; two data centers approved within sight of
the property in the last four years. Rising bills, plus noise, plus a transmission
corridor survey.

**What they ask:** "Does the Commonwealth think there's any number of these that's too
many?"

**This is the hard one.** We have a great deal to say about *terms* and nothing on the
record about *limits*:
- first-of-its-kind statewide **energy consumption tax** on data center energy use
- **SCC order** making data centers pay for transmission built exclusively for them
- **stricter emissions standards** on backup/diesel generators
- parameters on **noise pollution** and **water-related technology**
- **new tools for localities** to assess impacts on homes, schools, and farmland
- her stated intent to keep pressing for commonsense environmental standards

**Do not improvise past that.** There is no public position from this office on a
moratorium, a cap, or a pause, in either direction. The truthful answer to the Combses
is that the administration's approach is to make the industry pay its full costs and
meet strict standards, and to give *their county* better tools to weigh a project — and
that the county decision is not the Governor's to make. If pressed further, take it
down and route to policy.

## HOW TO USE THESE

Test any new energy language against all four. If it works for Denise and fails for
Marcus, it's a household message wearing a statewide costume. If it works for everyone
except Tanya, we've written a ratepayer message and forgotten the workforce leg — which
is a recurring failure mode in our energy drafts and the reason this document exists.
