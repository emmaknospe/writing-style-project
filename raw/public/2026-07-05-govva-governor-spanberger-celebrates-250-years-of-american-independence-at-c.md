WILLIAMSBURG, VA — Governor Abigail Spanberger yesterday joined Virginians from across the Commonwealth at historic Colonial Williamsburg — the political, cultural, and educational heart of revolutionary Virginia — to celebrate 250 years of American Independence.

In remarks at the celebration, Governor Spanberger chronicled Williamsburg’s prominent role on America’s road to independence, reflected on the enduring principles laid out by the Founding Fathers, and urged all Virginians to renew their commitment to civic participation and the ideals that inspired our nation. Earlier in the day, Governor Spanberger delivered keynote remarks at the Monticello Independence Day Celebration and Naturalization Ceremony — welcoming 75 new American citizens.

Read excerpts of Governor Abigail Spanberger’s remarks below:

—

Good evening, Williamsburg!

To the thousands of you gathered here on Palace Green — and to everyone watching at home across Virginia and across the nation — happy Fourth of July.

[...]

Virginia has been essential to our American story since before there was an America. Right here in Williamsburg — the revolutionary capital of Virginia — the notion of self-governance began taking hold among the hearts and minds of the Virginians who would lead in the founding of our nation.

On this Fourth of July, we celebrate our nation’s founding and all that makes our country extraordinary.

That we are a nation founded on ideals makes us unique the world over.

That we are a nation built by people bound together not by language or religion or background but in common pursuit of freedom makes us special.

That we are a place where generation after generation, it has been newcomers — dreamers, innovators, adventurers, those in search of safety or a new beginning — who have been the source of our strength makes us a beacon of light to so many.

[...]

In the Declaration of Independence, Thomas Jefferson boldly proclaimed that they would create a new nation based on ideals — that there are self-evident truths, that all men are created equal, that we are endowed by our creator with certain unalienable rights.

That among them are life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

He wrote that to secure these rights, they would create a new nation that would take on the extraordinary task of governing itself — deriving power from the consent of the governed.

In that declaration — and the documents that followed — our founders chose to believe in a new nation. A nation not built on or bound by language, religion, or fealty to kings or queens, but built on ideals. Unlike anything the world had ever seen.

And while we reflect on the audacity that was the Declaration of Independence — it is our Constitution that continued to breathe life into the idea of what America could become.

The purpose of this new nation was to form an ever more perfect union, and who would do it?

“We the People.” And just as our declaration left promises unrealized at the time, so too did our Constitution leave things undefined — who is the “We?”

I will leave it to the historians to ponder in detail and analyze the words in context or their meaning today, but quite simply put — ours is a nation unlike any other on earth because it has always been on us — we the people — to define our ideals and realize their promise.

[...]

And we are an ever expanding and vibrant array of people.

I saw that firsthand just this morning at Thomas Jefferson’s Monticello, where I participated in the naturalization ceremony of 75 new Americans.

People who woke up this morning as citizens of 35 different nations and who will go to sleep tonight citizens of the United States of America, bound by their choice to become Americans.

They spoke earnestly of what it meant to become an American.

One young woman thanked her father for listening when, as she recounted, in a moment of silent prayer, God told him to take their family to America.

One mother cried as she expressed her pride in becoming a citizen of our country — the country her son serves as a United States Marine.

One young man spoke with gratitude for his parents’ choice to escape war and come here to a place of extraordinary opportunity.

One man likened his naturalization to marriage — joking that after 20 years of loving the United States, it’s now official in the eyes of the law.

Like generations before them, they chose to come here for adventure, for love, for an education, and to pursue a dream that could only be possible in America.

They chose to come for survival, second chances, and to escape tyranny — in pursuit of a place where ideals and principles are what unite us. They chose America and to become part of that unparalleled “We the People.”

[...]

So, on this night — our 250th Fourth of July — I would encourage us to do what our founders did 250 years ago: believe in the ideals and promise of America.

And I would encourage all of us to do what those 75 new Americans did this morning: recommit ourselves to our extraordinary country.

Choose to give life to the words in our founding documents — in our own lives, in our communities, in our workplaces, and in our own individual acts of service to one another.

So that we the people may ensure the blessings of liberty and the beauty of our country for generations to come.

Happy Fourth of July, Virginia!

—
