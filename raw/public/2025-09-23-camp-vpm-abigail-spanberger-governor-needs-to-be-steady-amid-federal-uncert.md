By Jahd Khalil

After serving six years in Congress, Abigail Spanberger says she’s ready to be Virginia’s governor. Before becoming a congresswoman, she spent her career in federal law enforcement and as a case officer with the Central Intelligence Agency.

Now, she’s running against Republican Lt. Gov. Winsome Earle-Sears to determine who will become the commonwealth’s first female governor.

VPM News State Politics Reporter Jahd Khalil recently spoke to Spanberger at the Virginia Museum of History and Culture in Richmond about her campaign, as part of a series of conversations with the candidates running for Virginia’s statewide office this election cycle. (Links to interviews with the candidates can be found at the bottom of this story.)

Spanberger criticized federal cuts from Congress and the Department of Government Efficiency. She spoke about the importance of creating jobs, improving public education and addressing affordability for Virginians.

This interview has been lightly edited for style and clarity.

Jahd Khalil: Congresswoman Spanberger, thank you for talking with me. Why should Virginians pick you to be governor?

Abigail Spanberger: I’m asking Virginians all across the commonwealth to vote for me because I want to continue serving Virginia. We have a lot of challenges facing our communities, whether it’s challenges of affordability and housing, healthcare and energy, or our desire to get our public schools back towards being on track to be the best in the nation.

As we see a climate of of chaos and disruption coming out of Washington — whether it’s from the administration’s tariff policies or from bad policies coming out of Capitol Hill — we need a governor who’s going to be steady, focused on standing up for Virginia, putting Virginia first, doing right by our communities and working to address the things that keep so many families up at night: issues of costs and the future of our kids.

You’d be in charge of a huge bureaucracy if you win. I’m curious how your congressional experience is going to lend itself to being governor, and what your leadership style would be?

As governor, I will be attuned to asking the constant questions: What’s working, and how can we make that better? What’s not working, and how can we fix it?

If we look towards the administration of Medicaid as an example, because of the provisions from the so-called One Big Beautiful Bill, it will fall on the state to confirm and validate someone’s employment in order for them to continue to be eligible for Medicaid.

Now, currently, there’s a backlog in the system for that work validation, and the new federal requirements will require an even more frequent work validation. So, here’s an example of a place where we will need to create major efficiencies and a focus on effectiveness; otherwise, Virginians will lose healthcare.

Bringing that mindset to all of our state agencies, into the work of leading the state, is how I view the job and an example of the types of things I’m thinking about now.

How can you make life more affordable with so much funding going out of the system?

I’m looking at it in two different pieces. One will be the fiscal impact on the state because of either the One Big Beautiful Bill or the impact of DOGE — so many Virginians who’ve lost their jobs.

UVA is projecting that in 2026, Virginia will have no notable job growth. Over the past seven months, we have had increased rates of unemployment each month, so we’re already looking at challenges as it relates to tax revenue coming in — in addition to the One Big Beautiful Bill taking $26 billion out of our health care system.

Then there’s the separate priorities of lowering costs. The good news is that many of the priorities that I’m pursuing to lower costs are, in fact, themselves cost-neutral. Some of it is legislative efforts to remove some hurdles from localities that might, in the housing space, wish to have greater flexibility in the housing they create and build. In the healthcare space, ensuring that people can use the pharmacies of their choice, or that pharmacy benefit managers aren’t blocking markets and eliminating competition.

Many of the policies in my affordability plan are an issue of governance coming out of the state administration, or legislative priorities to push through the General Assembly that, in and of themselves, are cost-neutral.

How willing are you to find other revenue sources like changing or adding new taxes? There was a digital tax that was proposed that ended up not making it through, and there’s been longstanding discussion about making Virginia’s income tax more progressive.

When you look at Virginia’s tax system, we haven’t had updates to our tax system in many, many decades in a meaningful way. We also have some taxes on the books that have been there since the 1700s and the early 1800s, so principally what I’m looking at is: Where are we going to be on our fiscal trajectory?

We’ll know a lot more even at the end of September, when we start to see how many more people might have taken that early retirement from the DOGE process in addition to those who were fired. But looking towards the future, I am trying to determine what might be the revenue sources that might be additive, but also what, what taxes could we eliminate?

I’m looking at the car tax as one. It’s a complicated issue — I think that your listeners and viewers know that — but it is probably the most hated tax in all of the commonwealth. The key is how we can ensure the revenues are able to pay for our schools and our first responders and the requirements of our communities — but also that there is fairness and, as it relates to the car tax, not the yearly or biannual headache, depending upon the locality you live in.

Virginia determines how much a local school district needs by calculating its staffing needs, and that’s sort of unusual across the country. One alternative is to make that more student-based, funding districts according to a per-student cost. Is that something that you would be in favor of?

We’ve seen many districts or many states across the country employ that method. But specific to Virginia, there’s a JLARC study that looked at the student-based structure for funding schools, and made recommendations about that being the most effective.

In this moment of disruption, I think it’s a time where we need the governor to keep us steady, but also to look at places where we can make substantial improvements. I have been digging deep into both the JLARC study and examples of states that have moved to a student-based school funding structure.

A big part of affordability is energy. Data centers use tons of energy, and we have more and more of them across the state. The State Corporation Commission is already considering higher utility rates for Virginians. And there’s some speculation that the climate goals in the Virginia Clean Economy Act are going to make it hard to balance reliability and energy prices.

How do you think the Virginia Clean Economy Act should be revisited or reformed, if at all?

As it relates to the Virginia Clean Economy Act, I think that there’s a real need to to be clear: I support the legislation and the principles behind it.

I do think that there are areas where we can make some updates based on increased technology since that bill became law. Notably, battery storage capacity and capability was not what we know it to be today. So, putting into place some provisions that account for the strength of battery storage, that incorporate newer technologies, I think, is one important thing.

I’ve heard discussions of doubts as to whether we can meet the goals. So, do we just abandon it? I think that would be a mistake. It’s a question of revisiting how we can continue to meet those goals in a changing landscape, and recognizing the sort of dire circumstance for so many families of rising costs.

What we really need to do is generate more energy here at home. It’s exciting that Virginia is going to be home to the largest offshore wind installation that, ideally, should begin initial generation of energy in the new year. But we need to continue to demonstrate leadership in the area of energy generation — and also ensure that high-energy users, such as data centers, are paying their fair share.

Looking at the SCC case going through, I think it’s an important element to see if, in the end, we will have a separate rate for high-energy users, principally data centers.

In your energy plan, you talk about common-sense locations for solar sites. I think abandoned mines were one. Would you support legislation that opens those sites up by-right, instead of the current process of having to go through localities to site solar?

I think where we are right now is in a place where some of our localities that might be wishing to make decisions about solar may not have all the information.

As someone who served my whole time in Congress on the Agriculture Committee — knowing agriculture is Virginia’s No. 1 private industry, and knowing that there exists a great level of concern about taking agricultural lands out of production for the purpose of industrial solar — I recognize why many localities across Virginia might have started to say that they want to reject solar coming into their communities.

However, there are many locations across the commonwealth, such as abandoned mine sites or non-productive, non-agricultural lands, where we could bring forth solar, creating important revenue streams for localities — and, importantly, lowering the cost for communities surrounding those sites.

What I think the state needs to implement, and I would as governor, is a statewide strategy recognizing that the sites that solar energy developers are going to want to come to are sites where they can put that energy onto the grid. Location matters. Installation capacity and availability matters.

Frankly, this is a place where I think our localities need to be working in concert with the Virginia Economic Development Partnership to make clear that we are active participants in making communities and localities aware of the places and the resources that could be viable options — to bring that type of investment, and in doing so, allay some of the concerns that they may have related to loss of productive agricultural lands.

During this administration, there’s been a turn away from directly addressing the legacy and lasting effects of racism to a certain degree. How would you do that differently?

As governor, it will be my priority to ensure that we are teaching the full history of our commonwealth and of our country.

Looking at Virginia as a very specific example, we are the place where the ideas of the birth of our nation and our founding documents were created, were dreamed upon. We’re also the place where slavery first came to the shores of what’s now the United States of America. You can teach and you can make sure that people understand that history together and that one does not negate the other.

In fact, understanding the history of our country and the history of Virginia, especially as we’re coming up upon the 250th anniversary of our country, is essential. So, as governor, I would want to ensure that every kid in school understands the full history of who we are.

There was a bill to have African American history AP courses count towards some of the history requirements towards graduation. And that effort was not supported by the current governor. One of my daughters is taking that course, and she’s loving it, and she’s learning so much.

I think that it’s important that we recognize all aspects of our history — and allowing our students to explore the good and the bad, and the reality of our history, is how we truly learn from it.

Race has been a big part of the way that higher education institutions have experienced politics in the past several months, especially with the forced departure of UVA President Jim Ryan.

You would have the opportunity to appoint people to boards of visitors. How would this recent history affect who you were appointing?

As it relates to public universities, where the governor has the ability to appoint the full board, we’ve seen the challenges at the University of Virginia. We also saw the challenges at the Virginia Military Institute, where the board forced out the first Black superintendent — who, every year prior, had received commendations for the extraordinary work that he had done. Now, we’re seeing ongoing challenges at George Mason University.

The role of the governor is to appoint people who want to strengthen the universities that they will be serving on the board. It is not to drive any ideology or particular political goals of a governor or an administration. What I will be looking at in the decisions I’m making about board appointments is who wants to strengthen our public universities, who wants to make sure that they are having a long-term view for what it means to be successful.

That means bringing in students. That means being drivers of Virginia’s economy. That means graduating strong citizens into our community who might stay in Virginia, start businesses in Virginia, raise families in Virginia, — or, if they go someplace else upon graduation, reflect fondly on their time studying here in our commonwealth.

My goal is to appoint people who are dedicated to strengthening our public universities, full stop.

Can you talk about your plan to drive jobs to Virginia or expand the ones that are here?

This year, Virginia lost our place as the No. 1 state for business, according to CNBC. And notably, in their explanation of that change, one of the reasons for that decision was the loss of jobs because of the DOGE attacks — recognizing that the landscape has become a bit difficult in Virginia, and the rest of the country, because of DOGE, because of trade policy from the White House, the list goes on and on.

The role of the governor, I think, is to endeavor to make the state as steady as we possibly can, and to look at the types of things that businesses are looking for when they are looking to make investments.

What’s the strength of our workforce? Are they ready to take the jobs at whatever business might be brought to a particular community? What is the school infrastructure, if they’re looking to bring people to a community? And what’s housing like for our workforce? It’s infrastructure. It’s transportation capacity. Infrastructure includes electricity and broadband connectivity.

As governor, I will work tirelessly to make sure that we take every opportunity to strengthen the components that make Virginia the top place for businesses. Those things all go hand in hand when we’re looking to bring investment to Virginia and, ultimately, additional jobs.

A lot of the housing policies that you’re proposing are geared towards increasing supply. One was incentivizing bonding authority for localities. Can you explain what that means and how that is going to increase housing?

There’s a number of elements of our housing plan that put choice and ownership over expanding housing directly on localities.

As a Dillon rule state, sometimes our localities are limited in the things that they can do. So, to have a locality be able to underwrite and financially support new projects — or the components necessary to build in or bring forth a new housing project — is important, allowing localities to quickly rezone and turn over buildings that might have previously been in manufacturing into housing.

The types of flexibility that our localities can and should have, I think, can drive local-based solutions that meet the needs of a particular community. I recognize that it’s not a one-size-fits-all approach. But if you spend any time traveling across Virginia, so many of our communities are very different.

The solution for Abingdton is not the solution for Arlington, but ensuring that they both have the opportunities to really drive on the types of housing development or growth — whether it’s high-rises, whether it’s apartment buildings, whether it’s new residential developments — enabling localities to be able to do it and support them is a priority for me.

Every time we have an election, we always hear it’s the most important one ever. But obviously, there’s always stakes involved. Can you talk about what the stakes are in this election, and how that should influence people’s choice when they go to the ballot box?

I think the decision in this election is a consequential one, because there are very significant differences between the policies that I’m pursuing and the ideas that I’m putting forward and the ones that I am hearing from my opponent.

So many people are talking about issues of affordability. Not only was the first plan that I put out focused on affordability and lowering costs, but I also recognize the real dire circumstances of the position we may be facing into the future because of DOGE, because of tariffs, because of the so-called One Big Beautiful Bill.

You cannot contend with threats to your economy, you cannot contend with real fiscal issues, if you don’t even assess them as real — or if you are choosing, as I would say my opponent is, to put loyalty to a president and his talking points above the real economic impact on Virginians.

In addition to having a real plan to address issues of cost, I also want to put Virginia on paths towards strengthening our public schools, ensuring we have the best in the nation. And my opponent, for her entire career in politics, has focused on defunding public schools.

On issues of basic rights, I support the constitutional amendments that are moving forward to ensure restoration of rights for someone who’s been through our criminal justice system. Virginia is among the very worst in the nation for permanently disenfranchising people.

Virginia still has a ban on same-sex marriage in our state constitution, and I support removing that. My opponent has said she’s morally opposed to it.

And Virginia has a constitutional amendment moving forward related to codifying the standard of Roe v. Wade to ensure that women and their families and their medical providers can make their own independent decisions without undue influence from the government or from people in politics. And my opponent has also said she’s morally opposed to that.

On issues of policy, the choice is very different. But it’s also an issue of leadership at this moment where we see chaos coming out of Washington, whether it’s in policy or in divisive rhetoric.

I believe that Virginia needs a governor who wants to be steady and focused on ensuring that Virginia continues to be a great place to raise a family, a great place to make a business investment, to grow a business, to attract business or to retire.

I’m focused on the actual brass-tacks elements of how we do that, and I think that when you make the comparison side-by-side of who’s going to lead the commonwealth at this time — particularly when we see a real tension within our politics — I would argue that I am the the better choice to demonstrate that type of leadership.

It’s the background of service to the country that I bring from my time as an intelligence officer and a federal agent, but also it’s a demonstrated skill set that I brought to my time on Capitol Hill, when I was the most bipartisan member of Congress from Virginia. I was building out coalitions constantly and consistently to get things done to benefit the people I served in Congress.

I’m looking forward to this election. It’s an exciting time, and with early voting beginning, I hope that Virginians understand that we have an extraordinary opportunity to be among the first in the nation to go to the polling places. In 2025, it’s just Virginia and New Jersey with statewide elections. It’s a responsibility, but it’s also an exciting time.
