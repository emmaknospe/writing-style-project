By Drew Hansen

Virginia has fallen from the top spot in CNBC’s annual ranking of the top states for business, checking in at No. 4 this year.

Gov. Glenn Youngkin isn’t taking the demotion in stride.

The Republican on Thursday blamed new methodology from CNBC this year that includes “a new subjective metric that mistakenly ascribes substantial risk to Virginia from the federal government’s presence in the Commonwealth,” Youngkin wrote on social media.

CNBC said it added new metrics this year that gauge states’ risks from a trade war and a shrinking federal budget, while also enhancing some infrastructure metrics that measure how well states are delivering on power and data demands from companies. The media company said it develops its criteria and metrics in consultation with business and policy experts as well as the states themselves.

“It is our financial and economic strength that CNBC simply missed this year,” Youngkin wrote. “By the objective metrics, Virginia is still the best state to start or grow your business.”

He said CNBC failed to recognize “record” growth in the state’s private sector and job market — he cited 270,000 more Virginians working and 200,000 open or unfilled jobs — that he said has helped drive four consecutive years of record surpluses. Youngkin also said the state’s federal workforce is weighted toward defense and national security, which is getting more federal funding.

Youngkin also mentioned Virginia having its AAA bond rating reaffirmed as neighboring Maryland and D.C. have had theirs downgraded. He also suggested Virginia’s rainy-day fund is “among the best resourced in the nation.”

Youngkin has made habit of mentioning the state’s No. 1 ranking from 2024 at just about every economic development stop he makes across the state, from ribbon-cuttings to small meetings.

Abigail Spanberger, the Democratic nominee for governor, seized on the new ranking.

“Today’s rankings make clear that Virginia needs a Governor who will build a more resilient economy, leverage our Commonwealth’s strengths to attract new investment, prepare our workforce for 21st-century jobs, and stand up for our workers and small businesses in the face of ongoing threats,” Spanberger said in a statement.

Republican nominee Winsome Earle-Sears has not commented on the new ranking. In April, Earle-Sears, the state’s current lieutenant governor, was caught on tape dismissing the impact of job cuts in the state related to federal cuts.

“Unlike my opponent in this race, I won’t dismiss threats to our workforce, our economy, and Virginia’s reputation,” Spanberger said in her statement. “As Governor, I will always stand up to reckless policies that hurt our ability to attract new investments and retain our talent.”

The annual CNBC ranking measures the 50 states on 135 metrics (up from 128 a year ago) in 10 categories of competitiveness to determine which states are delivering effectively on the things that mean the most to business. The District is not included in the ranking.

Virginia earned high marks in the education, infrastructure and business friendliness categories, but fell three spots on its economy score to No. 14 this year and seven spots in the cost-of-doing-business measure to No. 31.

North Carolina claimed the top spot this year. It last earned the No. 1 ranking in 2023, when Virginia landed at No. 2.

Rounding out this year’s top five are Texas at No. 2, Florida at No. 3 and Ohio at No. 5.

Maryland came in at No. 32, down one spot from the year prior. The Old Line State earned low marks for the cost of doing business and cost of living. It earned high marks in the technology and innovation and education categories. Maryland ranked No. 22 in 2023.
