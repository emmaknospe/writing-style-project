By Gregory S. Schneider

Virginia Gov. Glenn Youngkin (R) has faithfully defended President Donald Trump’s tariffs and cuts to the federal workforce despite their economic harm to the commonwealth, but on Thursday that allegiance deprived Youngkin of a favorite talking point.

Virginia lost its coveted “Top State for Business” ranking from the CNBC financial news network, falling to No. 4 thanks to Trump administration action that the network said “hits The Old Dominion where it lives.”

Youngkin, a former private equity tycoon who has national political ambitions, quickly dismissed the demotion. “CNBC’s new methodology this year is thrown off by a new subjective metric that mistakenly ascribes substantial risk to Virginia from the federal government’s presence in the Commonwealth,” the governor said in a written statement.

CNBC said in its write-up about Virginia that it had to take the federal actions into account because “President Trump has begun to fundamentally change the relationship between the federal government and the states. … Virginia is already seeing a small downturn of sorts, as the Trump administration sets out to slash the federal workforce.”

Virginia has more federal workers than almost any other state, and its economy is uniquely tied to the federal government. The network pointed out that Virginia was one of only three states whose unemployment rates rose in May, though at 3.4 percent, the rate is still below the national average.

“With economic anxiety rising, Virginia’s economic situation is just shaky enough to take it down a few pegs,” the network said. North Carolina took the top spot on the list.

Youngkin counters that Virginia has drawn $121 billion in business investment or commitments during his administration, created thousands of jobs, and maintained its AAA bond rating. The state’s federal government jobs are “heavily weighted” for defense and national security, “which are getting more funding, not less … It is our financial and economic strength that CNBC simply missed this year,” he said in the statement.

The news immediately became fodder in Virginia’s closely watched election season, with contests for governor, lieutenant governor, attorney general and all 100 seats in the House of Delegates on the November ballot.

Virginians are “worried about the uncertainty caused by the chaos coming from Washington and the damage done to Virginia’s economy,” former congresswoman Abigail Spanberger, the Democratic nominee for governor, said in a statement after the CNBC announcement. “Unlike my opponent in this race, I won’t dismiss threats to our workforce, our economy, and Virginia’s reputation. As Governor, I will always stand up to reckless policies that hurt our ability to attract new investments and retain our talent.”

Youngkin is prevented from seeking reelection by term limits, but Republican gubernatorial nominee Winsome Earle-Sears is the state’s lieutenant governor and has often taken co-credit for the state’s economic success while she campaigns.

Her campaign spokeswoman, Peyton Vogel, on Thursday blamed the rankings drop on Democrats in the General Assembly, who she said had blocked Republican proposals such as ending taxes on tips and abolishing the car tax that would have further strengthened the state’s economy.

“This, frighteningly, is a harbinger of things to come if Democrats continue to stand in the way of smart economic policies that foster growth and opportunity,” Vogel said. “Our competitive advantage nationally has already been weakened with liberal control in the General Assembly. It’ll be hard working Virginians who pay the price if Abigail Spanberger is governor.”

John Reid, the GOP candidate for lieutenant governor, dismissed the rankings. “I’m sure all the editors in NYC know we have elections here this year so never underestimate political motivations in these media lists,” Reid said on X.

Earle-Sears had often touted the top ranking on the campaign trail. “We want to continue the successes of our administration,” she said at an event in Falls Church last month. “We are now the number one best state to do business. Think about that. … You get there by being deliberate, by having policies that create jobs, by having the policies that get rid of time-sucking labor relations and other regulations.”

At the same time, Earle-Sears, like Youngkin, has vigorously defended the U.S. DOGE Service’s efforts to cut the federal workforce and impose tariffs on trading partners by saying they are necessary steps to heal the nation’s economy and rightsize the federal government. While there might be some short-term pain, both have said, the state’s economy is strong enough to absorb the shock and help anyone who lost a job get back on their feet.

“I have lost a job,” Earle-Sears said at the Falls Church event. “I know what it feels like to lose a job, and so we’re trying to make sure that everybody has a job if they want one. If they want to become an entrepreneur, we can help them to do that. If they want to upskill from out of where they currently are, we can them do that, too. Leaders know how do this.”

Youngkin, who cut $900 million from the state budget this year to hold in reserve in case the Trump cuts cause financial problems, mounted a campaign-style tour in the spring to tout the idea that Virginia has plenty of jobs for anyone who was cut by the purges of DOGE, which stands for Department of Government Efficiency. He cited the state’s previous CNBC ranking as evidence.

“Virginia has jobs. Lots of jobs. What we’ve seen over the last three years is a commonwealth of Virginia that has absolutely reawoken as the best place in America to do business, stated by CNBC last summer,” Youngkin said in March at a jobs-related rally in Fredericksburg. As recently as Tuesday, as Youngkin held an event outside Richmond to celebrate what he said was a historic streamlining of the state’s business regulations, he touted Virginia’s status as “the best business state in America.”

He was not always so enamored of the CNBC rankings, which Virginia has topped more than any other state — six times — since the list debuted in 2007. Virginia was ranked No. 1 for business twice under his Democratic predecessor, Gov. Ralph Northam, in 2019 and 2021; the network issued no list for the pandemic-shutdown year of 2020. While campaigning for governor in 2021, Youngkin derided those ratings as misleading and masking underlying weaknesses in the state.

After he took office in 2022, the state fell to No. 3 on the list, then hit No. 2 in 2023 before regaining the top spot last year.

Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Virginia), who was the state’s governor when it led the first CNBC list, said Tuesday’s demotion “highlights that the chaos and uncertainty caused by President Trump’s tariffs, the slashing of federal funding, and the politicizing and hollowing out of the federal workforce are gut punches to Virginia’s economy.”

House Speaker Don Scott (D-Portsmouth), who is campaigning to build on Democrats’ 51-49 majority in the House of Delegates, said the ranking shows that Youngkin and Earle-Sears should be using their ties to Trump to advocate for Virginia’s interests. “They are so beholden to the MAGA culture and afraid of Donald Trump,” he said.

In Youngkin’s case, he added, that might be explained by his presidential ambitions — as evidenced by the governor’s plans to speak at an annual Republican fundraising event in the key primary state of Iowa on July 17 and at another such event in South Carolina in August.

Earle-Sears “has no reason to embrace the MAGA policies,” Scott said. “She’s running for governor. She needs to now show that she will stand up for Virginia families.”

For Youngkin to blame CNBC for its methodology, Scott said, is akin to blaming the teacher for not liking a grade. He added: “Republicans are in denial about what Trump is doing to the economy.”
