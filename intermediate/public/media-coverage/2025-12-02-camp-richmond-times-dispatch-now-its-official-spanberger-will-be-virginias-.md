By Dave Ress

They might have thought they’d been elected last month – but the victors in this year’s statewide and House of Delegates races didn’t actually win until Monday.

The State Board of Elections made it official, after a three-minute presentation and vote and then the signing by all five board members of 104 certificates of election – a process that took a lot longer.

But ensuring that Abigail Spanberger won election as governor, Ghazala Hashmi as lieutenant governor, Jay Jones as attorney general, 100 Virginians as members of the House, and local officials – such as Tony McFaddin, sheriff of the city of Lexington and Rockbridge County – was the result of weeks of work, and checking and rechecking the work of some 15,000 poll workers in 133 localities across the state.

“There’s three checks,” said Susan Beals, state commissioner of elections. “There’s election night, there’s the local electoral boards and then the state department” of elections.

They all involve making sure the votes counted for each candidate, along with write-ins and spoiled ballots, match the number of ballots cast. They also verify that those numbers tally with the separate reports required for voter turnout – the data that say in how many elections any Virginia voter has in fact voted.

The counting starts on election night, when precinct workers run paper ballots – the only kind used in Virginia – through tabulating machines.

Over the next several days after Election Day, local electoral boards review the tallies, precinct by precinct. They also do the arithmetic checks to ensure that the number of ballots cast for each candidate, along with spoiled ballots and undercounts, all equal the number of ballots issued and the number of voters recorded as voting in each precinct’s pollbook.

The local boards also review all provisional ballots – votes by people whose registration or eligibility to vote couldn’t be nailed down on Election Day. When the boards determine that a voter casting a provisional ballot was entitled to vote where he or she did, that vote is added to the total.

The local boards have 10 days to do these reviews and then forward their results – called Abstracts of Votes – to the state Department of Elections.

There, staff review the abstracts, along with the separate reports local boards do on turnout and listing of voters who cast ballots, to make sure that all the numbers add up correctly. If they don’t, state elections officials and local boards dig in to find out what causes the difference and correct the issue.

The result: a stiff, cream-colored certificate with a big gold seal and five signatures that tell candidates they’ve been elected.
