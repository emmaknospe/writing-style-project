By Michael Martz

PRINCE WILLIAM — Gov.-elect Abigail Spanberger got a close look Tuesday at the state fiscal outlook she will face when she becomes Virginia’s 75th governor on Jan. 17. It looks much different than the one Gov. Glenn Youngkin inherited almost four years ago.

Spanberger, a Democratic former congresswoman elected in a landslide Nov. 4, made an appearance at the House Appropriations Committee budget retreat, held at the George Mason University Science and Technology Campus near Manassas.

What she heard is that Virginia lawmakers will have less than $1.3 billion in state revenues available for discretionary spending. That is after paying for a record $3.2 billion increase in Medicaid costs over the next three years, an estimated $844 million in updated expenses for K-12 public education and $449 million in mandatory spending required under the federal budget reconciliation bill that President Donald Trump signed into law in July.

And that does not include an estimated $1.1 billion in potential cuts to state tax revenues under Trump’s package of federal tax cuts that the next governor and General Assembly will have to decide whether to adopt in the state tax code. It could be higher if legislators push for the state to conform its taxes to other provisions of the federal bill — such as eliminating the income tax on tips and overtime — that could cost an additional $1.2 billion.

“The forecast and all that we’ve seen today lays out some real challenges,” Spanberger said in brief remarks to the members of the House Appropriations and Finance committees.

She said her priority will be protecting Virginia’s AAA bond rating and balancing the budget to bring spending in line with revenues.

“I look forward to working with you all to meet the moment,” she said.

Youngkin, a Republican corporate executive who was new to government, took over in January 2022 with a two-year budget proposed by his predecessor, Gov. Ralph Northam, a Democrat, that was cushioned by a $2.6 billion surplus from the previous fiscal year. The surplus reflected big increases in federal emergency spending during the COVID-19 pandemic — especially for the state’s Medicaid program — and set the stage for $4 billion in tax breaks during the new governor’s first General Assembly session.

The state’s economy

One big unknown is what happens to the state’s economy, which continues to perform well despite uncertainty over the effects of Trump’s shifting tariff policy and cuts to the federal workforce and spending, especially in Northern Virginia and Hampton Roads.

In the first four months of the fiscal year that began July 1, state revenues have grown by $508.5 million, or 5.5%. They were powered by income taxes withheld from payroll and sales taxes, as well as better-than-expected estimated income taxes that investors and sole proprietors pay quarterly.

Youngkin said the state posted strong revenues in October despite the 43-day federal government shutdown that began on Oct. 1 and ended last week.

“While we saw some impact from the federal government shutdown on state revenue in October, there is potential for some softening in November revenues,” he said in announcing the revenues Friday.

“With that uncertainty behind us, we will continue to demonstrate — through prudent fiscal management, strong job growth and record capital investment from companies expanding in the Commonwealth — that Virginia remains financially stronger than she has ever been,” he said.

Youngkin will adopt a new revenue forecast for the budget he will introduce next month, based on the Moody’s baseline outlook that the Joint Advisory Board of Economists has endorsed and the Governor’s Advisory Council on Revenue Estimates will consider later this month.

The House budget presentation adopts the baseline forecast, but committee staff director Anne Oman said, “Virginia’s risks lean to the downside.”

The baseline forecast assumes that employment will grow a half-percent this fiscal year and slightly more in the next two years. It also assumes an unemployment rate of 3.4%, even though Virginia’s rate was 3.6% in August, the last month that the federal government had data to report before the shutdown. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics said that on Thursday it will release its first employment data since the shutdown began.

How many federal employees and contractors have left their jobs since Trump returned to office on Jan. 20?

“Nobody knows the answer to that question,” said Clark Mercer, executive director of the Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments and former chief of staff to then-Gov. Northam. “The data is horrible now.”

Northern Virginia outlook

Mercer presented a worrisome overview of the regional economy in Northern Virginia, which generates almost half of the state’s gross domestic product but relies heavily on the federal government for public and private employment. The U.S. Census estimates that 321,516 federal employees live in Virginia, including 172,499 in the Northern Virginia suburbs.

Federal contractors, who have been affected by canceled government funding since Trump took office, account for an additional 441,000 jobs in Virginia. Together, federal employees and contractors represent about 20% of the state workforce, and a much higher percentage in Northern Virginia.

Nationally, about 201,000 federal civilian employees have left the government this year through a combination of mass layoffs or voluntary resignations under pressure to reduce the workforce. Many of those workers remained on the federal payroll through Sept. 30, so the effects of the job actions in Virginia remain unclear.

Mercer reported that unemployment has risen by a percentage point in the past year in Northern Virginia localities, which also face a glut of vacant commercial office space, rising home sales as people leave for other states, declining hotel occupancy and slowing consumer spending.

Data centers, with the highest concentration in the world in Loudoun and Prince William counties, have become “overwhelmingly the brightest spot in the economy in Virginia, particularly Northern Virginia,” he said, while noting public concerns over their proliferation and the demand they place on electricity supply and bills.
